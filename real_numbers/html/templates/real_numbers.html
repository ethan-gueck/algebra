Real numbers obey a short list of properties: you can swap, regroup and distribute without changing the answer. Computers don't store real numbers, though. They store floating-point numbers, the nearest binary fraction to each value, and round after every operation. Most properties survive that. Some don't.

    When grouping changes the answer: a real case

    On paper, addition is associative: (a + b) + c and a + (b + c) are the same number. In floating point they often aren't. 0.1 is stored as 0.1000000000000000055…, and every addition rounds its result again, so changing the grouping changes which roundings happen. Subtraction is addition of a negative, so it inherits the same problem.

    I ran into this while calculating solar altitude, the angle of the sun above the horizon. The sine of the altitude is a sum of products of the latitude, the sun’s declination and the hour angle, and the altitude is the arcsin of that sum:

    sin(Hc) = cos(Lat)·cos(δ)·cos(ω) + sin(Lat)·sin(δ)   →   Hc = arcsin(…)

    Some of the arcsin results came back negative where that was not physically possible. Nothing was wrong with the formula. The floating-point arithmetic underneath it had rounded the sum to the wrong side of a boundary: a fundamental detail of how computers add numbers produced physically impossible outputs.

    How to guard against it

    • Don't compare floats for exact equality; compare within a tolerance.
    • Clamp a value to a function's domain before calling it: arcsin and arccos only accept −1 to 1.
    • Snap results within a tolerance of a physical boundary (here 0 and ±1) onto the boundary. The guarded row above does both.
    • Add long sums with compensated summation (Python's math.fsum), or at least in a consistent order.

    Exponent rules

    An exponent counts repeated multiplication: am is m copies of a multiplied together. Every rule below follows from counting copies. Pick a base a and whole-number exponents m and n; each rule is checked exactly and in floating point, where every multiplication rounds.

    Why the rules hold

    • Product: m copies of a times n more is m + n copies.
    • Quotient: dividing cancels n of the m copies, leaving m − n.
    • Power of a power: m groups of n copies is m·n copies.
    • Zero and negative exponents are the definitions that keep the quotient rule true: am ÷ am = 1 = a0, and a0 ÷ am = 1 ÷ am = a−m. Both divide by a, so they need a ≠ 0.
